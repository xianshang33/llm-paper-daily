## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Long-horizon language agents often possess local competence but fail to persist until explicit quantitative goals are met. Existing evaluations typically focus on final success flags, obscuring issues like progress loss, repetitive work, or premature stopping during the process.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current benchmarks (e.g., WebArena, SWE-bench) primarily measure final task success or local reasoning. They cannot distinguish between failures caused by poor local decisions and those caused by a lack of Quantitative Goal Persistence (QGP), such as agents falsely claiming completion after partial work or looping in duplicate submissions, which are hidden in traditional Pass/Fail metrics.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed PushBench**: A benchmark framework designed to measure and enforce Quantitative Goal Persistence (QGP) by using external verifiers to confirm the completion of a specific count of distinct valid items.
- **Challenge 1: Precisely quantifying and distinguishing agent failure modes (e.g., duplicate submissions, false completion, progress drift)**
  - **Difficulty**: Traditional evaluations only look at final outcomes, making it impossible to see if the agent is genuinely progressing or just spinning in invalid loops or self-deception.
  - **Solution**: PushBench introduces verifier-backed work units and repository artifact collection tasks that directly measure repetitive work, duplicate submissions, and false completions, making progress maintenance an auditable core metric rather than an implicit part of final success.
- **Challenge 2: Enforcing agents to maintain verified progress and stop only upon true completion in long-horizon tasks**
  - **Difficulty**: Frontier black-box agents (e.g., Claude Code, Codex CLI) show a sharp drop in success rates when facing large quantitative goals (e.g., 100 artifacts), revealing a gap between local competence and global persistence.
  - **Solution**: The paper compares various controller strategies, finding that a state-tracking retrieval controller achieves 69-78% success by eliminating duplicates, and a backlog-tracking work-unit controller achieves 25-50% success in settings where standard controllers fail completely, demonstrating the critical role of explicit state management and verification feedback.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: PushBench includes two controlled task families: QGP-RepoScan (repository artifact collection, isolating counting and retrieval) and QGP-DataOps-lite (small edits and structured updates accepted by deterministic verifiers). Black-box frontier agent evaluations were also conducted.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - **Controller Comparison**: The state-tracking retrieval controller achieved 69-78% success with no duplicate submissions. The backlog-tracking work-unit controller achieved 25-50% success in scenarios where standard and completion-gated controllers had 0% success.
  - **Frontier Agent Performance**: Claude Code (Sonnet 4.6) and Codex CLI (gpt-5.4) performed well on 50-artifact tasks but dropped to 3 out of 9 successes per condition on 100-artifact tasks.
- **Comparison**: Unlike traditional benchmarks, PushBench reveals that quantitative goals impose different reliability requirements than local task competence. Existing works often overestimate long-horizon capabilities because agents perform well on smaller scales but expose severe persistence defects when strict counting and continuous verification are required.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper highlights significant deficiencies in long-horizon LLM agents regarding "Quantitative Goal Persistence" (QGP), specifically their inability to continue working until an external verifier confirms sufficient valid outputs. By proposing the PushBench benchmark, the authors transform repetitive work, false completions, and progress drift from hidden failures into explicit metrics. Experiments show that even top-tier black-box agents fail significantly under high quantitative loads, while explicit state tracking and verification feedback mechanisms substantially improve persistence and reliability. This emphasizes that future agent systems must strengthen the maintenance of externally verified progress and global goal management, beyond just improving local reasoning capabilities.
