## 1. What the Paper Does
- **Background**: As Large Language Model (LLM) agents evolve from conversational interfaces to autonomous systems capable of complex tasks, tool usage, and inter-agent interaction, an "agentic web" with real-world consequences is emerging. A critical challenge arises: how to establish warranted trust when delegating tasks to unfamiliar agents in this open environment.
- **Why Existing Work Fails**: Current governance intuitions attempt to extend human-centric identity verification and reputation mechanisms (e.g., KYC, credit scores) to agents ("Know Your Agent"). The paper argues this analogy is fundamentally flawed. Traditional reputation systems rely on persistent identity, behavioral continuity, sanction sensitivity, and costly non-fungibility. However, LLM agents are ontologically "dissociative," composed of mutable modules, lacking the stable foundations required for these mechanisms to function.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Conceptual Framework**: The paper introduces the concept of "Dissociative Identity" for LLM agents, arguing that identity-based, ex post, sanction-driven governance (like reputation) is structurally inapplicable. It proposes a shift towards observability-based, ex ante, constitutive, protocol-driven behavioral harnesses.
- **Challenge 1: Lack of Identifiability and Persistence**. LLM agents are assemblages of mutable components (models, prompts, policies), resulting in fluid personas vulnerable to adversarial attacks. This ontological instability prevents the establishment of a persistent, unique identity, undermining the basis of any identity-bound reputation system.
- **Challenge 2: Ineffectiveness of Sanctions and Unpredictability**. Agents can swap modules or reset states, meaning they cannot "internalize" sanctions or bear long-term responsibility like humans. Drawing on dissociative identity disorder jurisprudence, the paper shows that agents lack the grounding for credibility and rehabilitability, rendering corrective feedback loops in reputation systems ineffective.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Nature of Work**: This is a theoretical analysis and policy position paper, not an engineering work with algorithmic implementations. Therefore, there are no traditional code deployments, training datasets, or quantitative metrics (e.g., accuracy, F1 scores).
- **Argumentation and Comparison**: The paper employs legal analogies (dissociative identity disorder jurisprudence) and system architecture analysis. Unlike existing works that focus on technical enhancements for agent security or Decentralized Identity (DID) solutions, this paper fundamentally questions the feasibility of migrating human "reputation/identity" paradigms to AI agents. It critiques current "identity-based governance" for assuming subject continuity, which contradicts the dynamic, assembled nature of LLM agents.
- **Proposed Direction**: Instead of experimental data, the paper offers a paradigm shift in governance: moving from ex post accountability via reputation to ex ante technical constraints based on protocol compliance and behavioral observability.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper highlights a critical ontological crisis in AI governance: the "dissociative" nature of LLM agents makes them incapable of supporting the persistent identity and liability required by traditional reputation mechanisms. The authors warn that blindly applying human-centric KYC or credit scoring models is not only ineffective but may obscure genuine security risks. It calls for abandoning the obsession with "agent identity" and developing new governance frameworks based on protocol constraints and behavioral observability to suit the dynamic nature of the agentic web.
