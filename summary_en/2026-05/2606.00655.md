## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) promise to solve complex tasks through collaborative intelligence. However, fundamental questions regarding their scaling behavior and intrinsic collective dynamics remain underexplored.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: Current frameworks (e.g., AutoGen, CAMEL, MetaGPT) focus on engineering implementations and specific applications, lacking isolated studies on the scaling effects of collaboration itself. Previous works often confound model heterogeneity and knowledge differences with collaboration mechanisms, failing to answer whether increasing agent count necessarily improves performance, and largely assuming that more agents always yield better results without theoretical backing.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed**: The Sequential Iterative Multi-Agent System (SIMAS) framework. A minimalist architecture centered on sequential inter-agent communication, designed to isolate the variable of collaboration and clearly observe scaling effects in homogeneous systems.
- **Challenge 1: How to isolate the pure scaling effect of collaboration?**
  - **Difficulty**: Existing systems typically mix agents with different capabilities or roles, making it hard to distinguish whether performance gains stem from model differences or pure collaborative redundancy.
  - **Solution**: SIMAS employs homogeneous agents (same base LLM) with only distinct role configurations (personality, beliefs, expertise) engaging in sequential iterative discussions, thereby treating "collaboration" as the sole variable for systematic study.
- **Challenge 2: What is the mechanism behind the non-monotonic performance change as agent count increases?**
  - **Difficulty**: Intuitively, more agents seem better, but there is a trade-off between collaborative synergy and coordination overhead. Long-context failure is often mistakenly cited as the primary cause of performance degradation.
  - **Solution**: Extensive experiments reveal that performance follows a pattern of diminishing returns. The study demonstrates that performance degradation stems primarily from coordination overhead rather than just long-context limitations, revealing that collective intelligence is an emergent property of strategic interaction design, not a guaranteed outcome of agent plurality.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Evaluation**: The authors conducted extensive experiments across diverse tasks and model scales. In SIMAS, n agents engage in T rounds of sequential discussion, generating responses based on accumulated conversation history, with the first agent synthesizing the final result.
- **Key Findings**:
  1. MAS performance does not scale monotonically with agent count but follows a pattern of diminishing returns.
  2. Effective MAS requires a sufficiently capable base LLM.
  3. Task type critically modulates the optimal number of agents.
  4. Performance degradation is mainly attributed to coordination overhead, not merely long-context failure.
- **Comparison & Generalization**: This scaling tendency generalizes across different interaction architectures, such as structured debate topologies. The work challenges the prevailing assumption that "more agents invariably lead to better performance" and offers practical guidance for designing efficient collaborative systems.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper systematically investigates the performance evolution of homogeneous multi-agent systems as the number of agents increases, proposing the SIMAS framework to isolate collaboration variables. It establishes MAS scaling laws: performance is governed by the trade-off between collaborative synergy and coordination overhead, exhibiting diminishing returns. Collective intelligence is not an inevitable product of agent quantity but depends on base model capability and strategic interaction design. This work provides a foundational scientific understanding of MAS collective behavior and corrects the misconception of blindly increasing agent counts.
