## 1. What the Paper Does
- **Background**: Tool-using LLM agents (e.g., coding agents) often fail not due to a lack of relevant text, but because decisive evidence is not selected, compressed, or surfaced at the moment of action. As context windows become a bottleneck, extracting decision-critical information from large retrieval sets is paramount.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current context selection methods rely on retrieval similarity or simple summarization, failing to treat "context selection" as a "decision intervention" problem. They cannot quantify whether a piece of evidence truly changes the agent's next action or improves expected outcomes, leading to key evidence being drowned in noise or ineffectively compressed.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed CICL (Counterfactual-Inspired Context Layer)**: A decision-aware context layer that transforms instance evidence into a context graph, routes judgments from various models (Opus, Qwen, Codex, etc.) through a shared eight-field schema, and packs high-utility evidence into typed "Memory Cards."
- **Challenge 1: How to quantify the actual utility of context for decision-making?**
  - **Difficulty**: Traditional methods struggle to distinguish "relevant but useless" information from "action-determining" key evidence, and judgment standards vary across models.
  - **Solution**: CICL introduces a counterfactual-inspired decision utility engine that scores units based on "action shift," "outcome uplift," "necessity," and "negative transfer risk." It decouples the decision signal from the judging model, allowing comparable auditing of frontier annotators, local surrogates, and lightweight rankers.
- **Challenge 2: How to efficiently compress and present key evidence under limited token budgets?**
  - **Difficulty**: Direct truncation or simple summarization loses critical constraints in logical chains, causing execution errors.
  - **Solution**: The "Memory Cards" mechanism rewrites high-utility evidence into structured units containing triggers, evidence, action hints, failure-if-ignored, and scope. This ensures that the most decision-valuable information is prioritized and surfaced within budget constraints.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Deployment**: CICL supports multiple backend judging models, including Claude Opus (assisted), Qwen API, Codex/GPT-5.5, and locally fine-tuned Qwen-QLoRA. All judgments are parsed into a unified format for reproducibility.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - On 50 file-retrieval instances from SWE-bench Verified, reranking BM25 top-50 candidates with Qwen3.6-plus increased Hit@1 from 0.58 to 0.78 and MRR@10 from 0.634 to 0.790.
  - All 2,500 judgments were parseable, demonstrating system stability.
  - Controlled diagnostics showed that at a budget of 120, CICL achieved an F1 of 0.620 on v1 and 0.425 on v3; removing the top-utility semantic unit collapsed F1 to 0.000, validating the criticality of selected evidence.
- **Comparison with Related Work**:
  - Compared to RepoBench-R summaries, memory cards still lag slightly in some scenarios, indicating compact rankers do not yet fully replace heuristics.
  - Unlike end-to-end code agent repair claims, CICL focuses on providing a reproducible measurement and selection layer.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper presents CICL, a decision-aware context selection and compression framework for tool-using LLM agents. By introducing counterfactual-inspired utility assessment and structured Memory Cards, CICL effectively addresses the absence of key evidence at action time. Experiments demonstrate significant accuracy improvements in code retrieval tasks and provide a general protocol that decouples judging models from decision signals. While challenges remain under extreme compression, its modular design and performance gains highlight its potential for building efficient context management layers for agents.
