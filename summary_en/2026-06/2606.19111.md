## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Multi-agent LLM teams often use patterns like debate, role specialization, or self-refinement to improve performance. However, these operate at the knowledge level, with coordination being implicit. Team science suggests leadership is contingent: it helps only under specific conditions, and capable autonomous teams may not need it at all.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current research focuses on engineering patterns to boost accuracy without rigorously measuring the specific conditions under which process-level coordination adds value. It lacks verification of whether these conditions align with predictions from team science, often treating coordination as a universal optimizer rather than a contingent variable.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a coordination control framework for multi-agent LLM teams based on team science**: Operationalized three classical leadership styles (transactional, transformational, situational) as controllers over a shared action vocabulary (explore, revise, accept, synthesize). Evaluated using behavioral signatures (majority lock-in, exploration, recovery) and per-action ablations.
- **Challenge 1: How to distinguish the value of coordination control from the mere presence of an action vocabulary?**
  - **Difficulty**: It is hard to prove that theory-derived rules are driving performance rather than just the introduction of specific actions.
  - **Solution**: Introduced a matched controller with the same actions but arbitrary rules. Results showed it recovered no better than majority voting, proving that the theory-derived rule, not the vocabulary itself, drives the improvement.
- **Challenge 2: Under what specific conditions does process-level coordination outperform simple interaction or voting?**
  - **Difficulty**: Controllers did not show universal accuracy gains across tasks and models, making it difficult to define their effective boundaries.
  - **Solution**: Proposed a "recovery-advantage" account tested with four boundary probes. Found that controllers only beat plain interaction when the round-0 majority is unreliable, the task is recoverable, and undirected interaction fails to repair it. These conditions map to contingency theory, explaining why null accuracy results are theoretically expected, not failures.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: Experiments conducted on a single backend across four task regimes and three open-weight model families. Controllers used explicit action sets instead of monolithic prompts for clean ablation.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - **Overall Performance**: No controller dominated in accuracy, consistent with the contingency view.
  - **Baseline Comparison**: Transactional control matched a shared round-0 vote within 1.3pp across all 12 (model, regime) combinations. Gains appeared only in the llama-4-scout social task where the round-0 majority was unreliable, benefiting situational and transformational controllers.
  - **Strong Baseline Comparison**: Against a stronger flat baseline, only the situational controller retained a gain (+8pp).
  - **Conclusion**: The largely null accuracy results are predicted by theory, indicating that leadership value is conditional.

#### Summary
This paper applies contingency leadership theory from team science to multi-agent LLM teams, revealing that the value of process-level coordination is conditional. It argues that coordination control should not be viewed as a tool for leaderboard dominance but as a contingent factor to be measured and mapped to theory. Significant performance improvements from theory-based coordination occur only when initial team consensus is unreliable and recovery is possible.
