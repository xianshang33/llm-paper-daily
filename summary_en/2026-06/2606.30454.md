## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used as proxies for human agents in social system simulations, computational social science, and behavioral experiments. However, it remains unclear when their behavior can be interpreted as a faithful proxy for human decision-making.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current validation methods often focus solely on outcome-level agreement (i.e., whether macro outputs are similar), ignoring the underlying behavioral patterns and decision processes. Recent studies suggest that apparent behavioral realism may be shallow, as LLMs may introduce systematic biases through compressed behavioral representations or rely on lexical associations rather than contextual reasoning, failing to capture true human heterogeneity and decision mechanisms.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed**: A direct empirical benchmark based on a large-scale networked Prisoner's Dilemma experiment. The study systematically compares nine open-weight LLMs against human participant data under identical interaction protocols, payoff structures, and network topologies, revealing a "macro-micro dissociation" in LLM-based social agents.
- **Challenge 1: Inconsistency between Macro Dynamics and Micro Mechanisms**. The difficulty lies in the fact that collective cooperation trends (e.g., early decline and later stabilization) may statistically match human data, masking fundamental differences in individual decision rules. The paper demonstrates that while selected models reproduce macro-level cooperation dynamics, this agreement does not extend uniformly to finer levels of behavior, proving the limitation of validating agents based solely on macro outcomes.
- **Challenge 2: Lack of Individual Heterogeneity and Conditional Cooperation Patterns**. The challenge is that LLM populations underestimate individual-level heterogeneity and generate conditional cooperation patterns different from those observed in humans. By introducing a fraction of random agents, the study shows that while some aspects of micro-level agreement improve, the mismatch in decision rules persists, confirming inherent defects in LLMs' ability to simulate complex, heterogeneous human decision logic.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: Nine open-weight LLMs were selected as agents and compared with human data in a large-scale networked Prisoner's Dilemma experiment. The study strictly controlled variables to ensure LLM agents used the exact same interaction protocols, payoff structures, and network topologies as human participants.
- **Evaluation Results**:
    - **Macro Level**: Some LLM models successfully reproduced key macro features of human cooperation dynamics, including the early decline and subsequent stabilization of cooperation rates.
    - **Micro Level**: LLM populations significantly underestimated individual behavioral heterogeneity. Furthermore, the conditional cooperation patterns generated by LLMs differed markedly from those observed in humans.
    - **Robustness Test**: Adding a fraction of random agents to the LLM population improved certain micro-level consistency metrics but failed to resolve the fundamental mismatch in decision rules.
- **Comparison**: Unlike previous studies that focused only on aggregate outcome consistency, this paper argues that such macro-level agreement is misleading. While related work might consider LLMs effective human proxies due to good performance on aggregated data, this study proves that this view ignores underlying mechanistic differences, emphasizing the necessity of multi-granular validation.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper reveals a significant "macro-micro dissociation" in LLM-based social agents: collective outcomes can appear human-like even when the underlying behavioral distributions and mechanisms are not. The findings suggest that validating LLM agents as reliable human surrogates requires comparisons across aggregate dynamics, individual heterogeneity, and context-dependent decision rules, rather than relying on outcome-level agreement alone. This offers important warnings and methodological recommendations for future social simulations and policy deductions using LLMs.
