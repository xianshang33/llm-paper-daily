## 1. What the Paper Does
- **Background**: Large Language Models (LLMs) and agentic AI systems have evolved from natural language tasks to using external tools for planning, retrieval, and action in technical domains. In smart grids, recent works apply agentic schemes to forecasting, optimization, and control by wrapping trusted solvers behind language interfaces.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: The literature lacks a unified approach to designing and evaluating these systems. Key issues include LLMs producing numerically plausible but physically infeasible outputs, inconsistent evaluation protocols across tasks, and implicit boundaries regarding what the model should versus should not compute.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a "Solver-Grounded" Design Principle**: A numerical result is reported only if it originates from a trusted tool and passes explicit verification.
- **Challenge 1: Physical Infeasibility and Hallucinations in LLM Outputs**. LLMs often generate values that violate physical constraints, making them unsafe for grid control. The paper addresses this by positioning the LLM as an orchestrator rather than a calculator, forcing it to invoke verified external solvers (e.g., CVXPY, power flow solvers) and using a verification gate to ensure physical compliance.
- **Challenge 2: Lack of Unified Evaluation Standards and Role Definitions**. Existing studies lack clear role definitions for agents in power grids and use varied metrics. The paper proposes a four-group evaluation framework (Task Utility, Solver-Grounded Correctness, Faithfulness & Safe Failure, Cost & Latency) and establishes a clear division of labor: the agent orchestrates/retrieves/explains, trusted tools compute, and the verification gate decides what is reported.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Case Studies**: The principle is instantiated in four scenarios: wind power forecasting, EV charging scheduling, power flow analysis, and contingency diagnosis.
- **Comparison**: Each case compares an "LLM-only" baseline against its "solver-grounded" counterpart using identical data and metrics.
- **Key Results**:
    - **EVAgent**: In EV charging scheduling, it reproduced the CVXPY optimum and reduced unmet energy by 7.5–9.5× compared to the LLM-only baseline.
    - **GridDebugAgent**: In contingency diagnosis, it repaired 17 out of 39 cases and reduced total violations by 52.3%.
- **Evaluation Framework Application**: The proposed framework demonstrated that the solver-grounded agentic systems significantly improved physical correctness and task utility while maintaining controlled latency and costs.

## 4. Conclusion
This tutorial systematically reviews the building blocks (prompting strategies and agentic architectures) of LLM and agentic AI systems for power systems. Its core contribution is establishing the "solver-grounded" design principle, where LLMs act as planners and orchestrators while delegating numerical computation to trusted tools. Case studies confirm that this clearly divided, verification-enabled agentic system significantly outperforms pure LLM methods in physical feasibility and task performance. The proposed unified evaluation framework provides a standardized benchmark for future research on agentic AI in smart grids.
