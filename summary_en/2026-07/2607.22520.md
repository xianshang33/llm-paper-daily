## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Adding procedural skills to LLM agents is typically evaluated by average improvement in task success. However, this metric hides a significant cost: skills can also degrade performance.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: Current evaluation methods focus only on net gain, failing to distinguish between tasks fixed by skills and tasks broken by them. Existing methods for detecting harmful skills only act when a skill is invoked, ignoring the impact of skill descriptions present in the context even when not called. They lack explanatory power for why regressions occur, potentially leading to the discard of useful skills.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Paired Decomposition Evaluation Framework**: Instead of relying solely on aggregate improvement, the paper decomposes pass-rate changes into "Gains" (failed without skills, succeeded with) and "Regressions" (succeeded without skills, failed with).
- **Challenge 1: Quantifying and Explaining Negative Regression Effects**. The difficulty lies in traditional metrics masking regressions and the inability to distinguish between skill ineffectiveness and interference. By comparing nearly 6,000 runs, the paper finds that top-performing skill libraries excel primarily by regressing less, not by gaining more. It identifies three regression mechanisms: (i) Skill-description osmosis (behavior change due to mere presence in context); (ii) Grounding displacement (procedure overrides input interpretation); and (iii) Verification displacement (procedure suppresses output checks).
- **Challenge 2: Identifying Root Causes of Persistent Failures and Correcting Evaluation Bias**. The challenge is that existing skills overemphasize procedural guidance (the least frequent source of failure) while under-supporting grounding and verification (the dominant sources of error). Trace analysis reveals that many regressions and persistent failures are recoverable through better grounding and verification. The paper re-graded 226 task conditions, showing that reliability depends more on grounding and verification than on the choice of procedural skills.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Experimental Setup**: Conducted 5,832 task-condition runs across two office automation benchmarks and three model-harness stacks. Only the skill library varied within each stack.
- **Key Results**:
    - Observed 324 regression transitions, offsetting 59% of the 553 gross gain transitions. This highlights that regressions are substantial and cannot be ignored.
    - Top-performing skill libraries outperformed others primarily by having fewer regressions, not by achieving more new successes.
    - Analysis shows existing skills overly focus on procedural guidance, neglecting grounding and verification, which are the main sources of remaining errors.
- **Comparison with Related Work**: Unlike systems like Trace2Skill or EvoSkill that optimize for average success rate, or methods that simply remove harmful skills without explanation, this paper provides fine-grained attribution. It explains "why" skills cause failures and demonstrates that enhancing grounding and verification can recover many failed cases.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper argues that evaluating procedural skills for LLM agents requires decomposing net effects into gains and regressions, rather than relying on average success rates alone. It identifies three major regression modes (description osmosis, grounding displacement, verification displacement) and finds that agent reliability depends more on the quality of grounding and verification than on the procedural skills themselves. Future skill design and evaluation should focus on minimizing regressions, particularly by strengthening grounding and verification steps.
