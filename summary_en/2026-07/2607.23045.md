## 1. What the paper did
- **Background**: AI applications in science are evolving from knowledge reasoning and plan generation to end-to-end autonomous scientific research. This requires AI agents to not only propose hypotheses but also execute reliable physical actions in robotic laboratories and adapt based on experimental evidence.
- **Why existing work falls short**: While current AI agents show increasing autonomy in scientific reasoning and planning, and robotic labs enable automated closed-loop experiments, there is a lack of systematic, quantitative assessment of how reliably AI agents can translate scientific intent into executable actions in robotic labs. Specifically, ensuring actions comply with hardware constraints (instruments, parameters, samples) and maintaining plan coherence and adaptability across multiple experimental rounds in modular, heterogeneous robotic labs remains an unsolved challenge.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a** physical-world testbed based on a modular robotic chemistry laboratory to conduct end-to-end stress tests on LLM-driven scientific agents, making scientific agency capabilities (physical executability and evidence-driven replanning) measurable.
- **Challenge 1: Low physical executability**. The difficulty lies in LLM-generated plans often ignoring specific laboratory hardware constraints (e.g., instrument interfaces, container formats, operational parameters), rendering most plans unexecutable. The paper addressed this by building a standardized robotic lab with 45 modular workstations, exposing hardware capabilities as machine-readable skills. Through 4,608 trials, it quantified the workflow executability rate under strict constraints (only 3.3% of trials produced expert-assessed executable workflows, with the best system achieving 28.1%).
- **Challenge 2: Insufficient long-horizon planning and evidence-driven replanning**. The difficulty is that agents struggle to perform workflow-level replanning or redesign analytical methods when receiving experimental feedback, limiting themselves to local adjustments. The paper revealed through multi-round experiments that even the longest executable workflows contained only 44 steps, and no workflow-level replanning was observed across five rounds, highlighting limitations in long-horizon planning and deep adaptation.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation and Deployment**: The team built a general-purpose robotic catalysis laboratory with 45 modular automated workstations (covering liquid handling, solid weighing, centrifugation, drying, electrochemical, photocatalysis, high-T/P reactions, and various spectroscopic/chromatographic characterizations). These stations are connected via standardized container formats and robotic gripper interfaces, with operations encapsulated as machine-readable skills.
- **Evaluation Results**:
    - Out of 4,608 trials, only 3.3% resulted in expert-assessed executable workflows.
    - The best-performing system achieved a 28.1% executability rate.
    - Long-horizon planning remains challenging: only three executable workflows exceeded 30 steps, with the longest being 44 steps.
    - Adaptability: In five rounds of iterative experiments, experimental feedback only triggered local adjustments; no workflow-level replanning or analytical method redesign was observed.
- **Comparison**: While the paper does not provide a detailed table comparing specific SOTA models, it explicitly states that previous studies lacked such systematic quantitative assessment. This study offers a more comprehensive assessment of deployment readiness by revealing capability boundaries and failure modes, surpassing prior works that focused merely on reasoning or single-task success rates.

## 4. Conclusion
This study systematically quantifies the scientific agency capabilities of AI agents in the physical world by conducting large-scale stress tests on LLM-driven agents in a highly modular robotic chemistry laboratory. The results indicate that state-of-the-art LLM agents still have significant deficiencies in translating scientific intent into physically constrained executable actions, particularly in long-horizon planning and evidence-based deep replanning. The study not only provides an evidence-based assessment of deployment readiness but also offers a diagnostic framework and direction for improving the development of physically grounded autonomous scientific research agents.
