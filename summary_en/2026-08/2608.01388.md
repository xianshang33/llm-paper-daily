## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Runtime safety monitors based on Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and Finite State Automata (FSA) are increasingly used to intercept unsafe tool-call sequences in LLM agents. However, empirical evaluations show drastic performance disparities: the same monitor achieves 68-75% attack coverage on some model architectures but near-zero coverage on others.
- **Why Existing Work Failed**: Standard engineering responses, such as collecting more training data or tuning hyperparameters, fail to address this issue because the problem is structural, not engineering-related. Previous works could not explain these disparities using model capability scores, training data, or prompt design. The core issue is that fixed-invariant FSA monitors are fundamentally bounded by the concentration of the attack distribution, which cannot be altered simply by adding more data.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed an "Entropy-Coverage Bound" theory and a pre-deployment entropy test**: The paper proves that the recall of any fixed-invariant FSA monitor is upper-bounded by the concentration of the attack distribution and introduces a method to predict monitor coverage from small attack samples.
- **Challenge 1: Explaining the vast performance disparity of monitors across different architectures**. The difficulty lies in the lack of a theoretical framework linking monitor performance to attack characteristics, as traditional metrics like model capability scores show no correlation. The paper solves this by proving that monitor recall is bounded by the sum of probabilities of the top-k most frequent attack patterns. Thus, low Shannon entropy (concentrated attacks) allows high recall, while high entropy (dispersed attacks) makes high recall impossible for any tractable invariant set.
- **Challenge 2: Predicting monitor effectiveness before deployment**. The challenge is that evaluating monitor coverage typically requires extensive post-deployment testing. The paper addresses this by introducing an architecture-agnostic pre-deployment entropy test. By calculating the entropy of a small sample of attack trajectories, developers can accurately predict the monitor's coverage, enabling informed selection of monitoring strategies prior to production deployment.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Experimental Setup**: Empirical validation was conducted across eight frontier LLM architectures, analyzing 847 adversarial trajectories.
- **Key Results**:
    - GPT-class and DeepSeek backends exhibited highly concentrated attack distributions (H ≈ 0.24 bits; one pattern covers 96% of attacks), explaining their high monitor recall (68-75%).
    - Gemini variants produced high-entropy attack distributions (H ≈ 2.81 bits; 7 distinct clusters each covering ≤7%), explaining their near-zero recall (6-13%). This result remained invariant even with architecture-matched retraining.
    - Entropy accounted for 76% of the variance in monitor coverage (Pearson r = -0.87, p = 0.005), with robust correlations under leave-one-out analysis (r ∈ [-0.91, -0.82]).
- **Comparison**: Unlike Elo capability scores, which showed no significant correlation with monitor coverage, attack distribution entropy proved to be a strong predictor. This demonstrates that entropy is a more critical factor than model capability in determining the efficacy of FSA-based monitors.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper identifies the fundamental theoretical reason for the failure of LTL/FSA-based safety monitors in certain LLM contexts: their efficacy is strictly bounded by the entropy of the attack distribution. Low-entropy distributions permit high coverage, whereas high-entropy distributions render fixed-invariant monitors ineffective, a limitation that cannot be overcome by standard engineering optimizations. The proposed entropy-coverage bound and pre-deployment test provide a scientific basis for selecting and applying runtime monitors, emphasizing the necessity of assessing attack distribution characteristics before deployment.
