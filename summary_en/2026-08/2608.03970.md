## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Human input to LLMs primarily comes via typing or speaking, each introducing distinct noise signatures: orthographic noise for keyboards and disfluency plus AI-driven restructuring for voice transcription.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Prior studies largely focus on benchmarking, establishing that noise lowers scores without explaining the mechanisms or providing actionable guidance. Crucially, they ignore the modern "LLM-rewritten" transcription layer, failing to measure the cost of structural reforms introduced by AI dictation tools.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed HIVE (Human Input-Variation Engine)**: A comprehensive suite of voice transcription and QWERTY keyboard perturbations designed to evaluate model robustness against real-world input variations.
- **Challenge 1: Unclear mechanisms of how different modalities impact performance**. Difficulty lies in distinguishing the specific costs of structural changes vs. simple noise. The paper finds that voice perturbations cause greater accuracy drops than keyboard errors, driven by structural transcription changes rather than fillers. The root cause for both is token destruction: losing original tokens hurts performance, while adding new ones has minimal cost.
- **Challenge 2: Ineffectiveness of lightweight adaptation and divergent effects of reasoning budgets**. Difficulty lies in mitigating these errors efficiently. The study shows that lightweight fine-tuning cannot train away these perturbations. Furthermore, increasing the "thinking budget" almost fully recovers performance for keyboard inputs but leaves voice inputs unaffected, even worsening performance for compressed speech, highlighting a fundamental modality gap.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Evaluation**: The authors evaluated multiple instruction-tuned models using HIVE, comparing voice transcription perturbations (including disfluencies and AI rewrites) against QWERTY keyboard perturbations (e.g., neighbor-key substitutions, dropped characters).
- **Key Findings**:
    1. Voice transcription perturbations lower accuracy across all models, with structural changes being the primary driver.
    2. Keyboard perturbations are less costly; models absorb significant noise before accuracy degrades.
    3. Performance drop is primarily caused by the destruction of question tokens, not the addition of noise tokens.
    4. The performance gap between modalities exists only in tasks requiring answer construction/deduction, not in multiple-choice questions.
    5. Harm is not solely due to test-set contamination.
    6. Lightweight adaptation fails to mitigate these issues.
    7. Increased reasoning compute recovers keyboard channel performance but fails for voice channels.
- **Comparison**: Unlike prior work that treats noise as a monolithic benchmark metric, this study dissects the structural impact of noise and reveals the asymmetry in how LLMs handle typographical vs. transcriptional errors, particularly under increased reasoning loads.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper presents a comprehensive study using HIVE to analyze the impact of voice and keyboard input perturbations on LLMs. It reveals that voice input is more detrimental due to structural rewriting and token loss. Crucially, it demonstrates that while increased reasoning compute can compensate for keyboard noise, it fails to improve robustness for voice inputs, indicating a fundamental asymmetry in current LLM architectures' handling of multimodal natural noise that cannot be solved by simple fine-tuning or scaling inference.
