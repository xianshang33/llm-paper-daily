## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: As AI agents are increasingly deployed in workplaces, organizations need taxonomies to identify and classify the socio-technical risks they introduce. However, existing AI risk taxonomies are often too broad or focused on technical failures, failing to capture job-specific risks introduced by agents.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: Current taxonomies do not adequately address socio-technical risks emerging from the dynamic interaction between AI agents and their social context (humans, organizations, institutions), leaving many workplace-specific risks unnamed and untracked.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Multi-layer Framework and Workplace AI Agent Risk Taxonomy**: Developed a framework modeling three core components (agents, goals, environment) and their interactions, leading to a 15-category taxonomy specifically for workplace AI agent risks.
- **Challenge 1: How to systematically generate realistic risk scenarios specific to diverse job tasks?**
  - **Difficulty**: Generic risk models struggle to map onto varied occupational tasks, and manual enumeration is costly and incomplete.
  - **Solution**: Embedded the multi-layer framework into structured prompts applied to 2,078 job task descriptions from the O*NET database, generating 8,356 risk scenarios labeled by severity and deployment mode (automation/augmentation). Validated plausibility with 45 workers and an independent LLM judge.
- **Challenge 2: How to accurately distinguish risk profiles across deployment modes (Automation vs. Augmentation) and assess usability?**
  - **Difficulty**: Conventional wisdom may assume "augmentation" is inherently safer than "automation," and there is a lack of comparative usability data for workplace-specific taxonomies.
  - **Solution**: Empirical analysis revealed augmentation is not inherently safe (risk of skill erosion). User studies showed workers found the new taxonomy easier to use than two other baselines, preferring it in 64% of non-tied comparisons.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Data**: Generated 8,356 risk scenarios from 2,078 O*NET job tasks using structured prompts. Validated with 45 workers across 10 job roles and an independent LLM judge.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  1. **Risk Distribution**: "Erroneous Agent Actions" accounted for the largest share of scenarios and the highest concentration of severe risks, often arising at the human-agent boundary.
  2. **Deployment Mode Differences**: Automation is mainly associated with organizational risks (operational failures, financial losses), while augmentation is linked to worker-centric risks (capability erosion, psychological/social risks).
  3. **Skill Erosion**: Augmentation is not inherently safe; overreliance can gradually erode worker skills and oversight.
- **Comparison with Related Work**: In comparative tasks, workers found the proposed taxonomy easier to use than two other risk taxonomies (including a recent generative AI risk taxonomy) and preferred it in 64% of non-tied comparisons.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper demonstrates that workplace AI agent risks stem not only from the agents themselves but also from how humans collaborate with them and how they are deployed. By providing a specialized framework and taxonomy, the study highlights distinct risk profiles for automation versus augmentation, emphasizing the need for carefully designed human-AI collaboration to ensure safer workplaces.
