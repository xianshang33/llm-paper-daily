## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Multi-agent Large Language Model (LLM) systems aim to enhance reasoning by decomposing tasks among specialized agents. However, the mere presence of multiple agents does not inherently guarantee coherent reasoning or alignment with task objectives.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: Current evaluation methods rely heavily on output-level metrics (e.g., accuracy), which indicate when a system fails but not why it fails at the discourse level. The complexity of multi-round reasoning makes it difficult to understand failure modes and make targeted improvements to agent prompts.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Diagnostic-to-Redesign Loop using Quantitative Ethnography (QE)**: The paper introduces a framework utilizing Epistemic Network Analysis (ENA) to model and analyze discourse patterns in multi-agent debate systems, guiding prompt optimization.
- **Challenge 1: How to quantify and identify specific interaction patterns leading to success or failure?**
  - Difficulty: Traditional frequency-based statistics fail to capture structural connections between discourse elements, making it hard to distinguish logically different interactions that appear similar superficially.
  - Solution: Applied ENA to represent meaning as patterns of connections among coded discourse elements rather than individual frequencies. This allowed precise modeling of structured agent exchanges, identifying essential structural differences between correct and incorrect decision-making processes.
- **Challenge 2: How to translate diagnosed discourse patterns into effective system improvements?**
  - Difficulty: Bridging the gap between abstract discourse analysis results and concrete engineering implementations (prompt modifications), while verifying if changes truly alter underlying reasoning mechanisms.
  - Solution: Established a "diagnosis-to-redesign" loop where insights from ENA (e.g., correct decisions rely more on rubric-grounded argumentation) were translated into targeted prompt revisions. The effectiveness was validated by comparing both output accuracy and discourse pattern shifts before and after revision.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Experimental Setup**: Used automated essay scoring as a testbed, implementing a five-agent Toulmin-Structured Multi-Agent Debate (TSMD) system with roles: Claim, Grounds, Warrant, Rebuttal, and Judge.
- **Diagnostic Findings**: In the initial system, correct scoring decisions were characterized by rubric-grounded justification, agreement, and elaboration. Incorrect decisions featured extended proposition-challenge-response exchanges with weaker ties to rubric criteria.
- **Results after Redesign**: Revising agent prompts based on these findings improved exact scoring accuracy from 27.78% to 40.28%.
- **Comparison**: Unlike baseline methods focusing solely on final output accuracy, this approach not only improved performance but also demonstrated via ENA that the discourse patterns of previously incorrect debates shifted towards those of correct ones, becoming nearly indistinguishable. This highlights the method's advantage in interpretability and actionability.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper proposes a new paradigm for diagnosing and improving multi-agent LLM systems using Quantitative Ethnography and Epistemic Network Analysis. By deeply analyzing discourse interaction patterns, the researchers successfully identified structural causes of reasoning failures and significantly enhanced system performance through targeted prompt redesign. This method transcends traditional black-box evaluation, offering an interpretable and actionable pathway for understanding and optimizing the reasoning mechanisms of complex multi-agent systems.
