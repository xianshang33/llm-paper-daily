## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Interactive simulations using LLM agents are increasingly used to evaluate market policies, generating economic metrics like prices, profits, and welfare.
- **Problem with Existing Work**: Current evaluations suffer from construct validity failures. Simulated economic outputs may appear valid but fail to instantiate the claimed economic behaviors. Previous implementations often confounded the effect of "guardrails" with implementation asymmetries (e.g., different offer schemas for controlled vs. uncontrolled groups) and ignored the high stochastic variance of LLM generations, leading to misleading causal claims.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a** "Construct-Validity Contract" for agent-market evaluation, which separates incentive validity, protocol isolation, stochastic stability, and welfare accounting, returning "INVALID" or "INCONCLUSIVE" before allowing substantive policy claims.
- **Challenge 1: Protocol Isolation Failure**
  - **Description & Difficulty**: Initial experiments used different input/output schemas for guarded and unguarded agents, making it impossible to distinguish whether improvements came from the guardrail rules or merely from a more user-friendly interface. This confounding variable invalidates causal inference.
  - **Solution**: The authors fixed the offer schema and buyer chooser across conditions. This correction reduced the reported welfare gains from +87.4 to +7.2 (or negative values), demonstrating that the original results were driven by protocol bias rather than the guardrails.
- **Challenge 2: Stochastic Instability and Incentive Validity Issues**
  - **Description & Difficulty**: LLM outputs are highly stochastic, and agents may not faithfully execute economic incentives (e.g., profit maximization). Single-turn samples do not capture generation variance, and seller responses to profit prompts were found to be non-monotonic.
  - **Solution**: The study employed multiple generations per profile with bootstrap confidence intervals, revealing that generation residuals accounted for 49.9% of variation. Scripted positive controls showed that guardrails only create welfare when sellers are explicitly programmed to force inefficient bundles; otherwise, they mostly redistribute or reduce welfare.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: A multi-turn buyer-seller testbed for configurable hotel transactions was built using Qwen2.5 models (1.5B–14B). Two guardrails were tested: blocking personalized extraction and preventing forced optional components.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - **Initial Findings**: Reported significant welfare gains (+87.4, +35.0, +28.8).
  - **Controlled Findings**: After fixing schema and choice procedures, contrasts dropped to +7.2, -13.9, and +23.8.
  - **Stability Analysis**: For 14B models, single-generation effects averaged +229, but averaging three generations reduced this to +37.6 with a 95% bootstrap interval of [-34.2, 109.3], indicating statistical insignificance.
  - **Incentive Check**: Increasing profit pressure in prompts resulted in lower profits than default prompts, indicating a failure in incentive alignment.
- **Comparison**: Unlike traditional economic simulations or naive LLM evaluations that accept surface-level metrics, this work demonstrates that without rigorous construct validity checks (protocol isolation, stochastic control), policy recommendations derived from LLM agent simulations can be fundamentally misleading.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper exposes construct validity traps in LLM agent commerce evaluation through a forensic case study. It demonstrates that apparently effective market guardrails become negligible or negative when implementation biases and stochastic variance are controlled. The core contribution is a rigorous evaluation contract emphasizing that incentive validity, protocol isolation, stochastic stability, and welfare accounting must be verified before drawing policy conclusions. The study does not claim guardrails are ineffective, but rather that their apparent value is unidentified until the simulated agents and protocols pass these strict validity checks.
