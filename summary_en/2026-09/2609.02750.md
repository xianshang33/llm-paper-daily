## 1. What the Paper Does

### Background
Multi-agent LLM systems typically employ an "orchestrator-worker" architecture, where an orchestrator decomposes tasks and workers execute them, improving through textual reflection. Despite strong empirical results, there is a lack of a unified theoretical account for coordination mechanisms, memory improvement processes, and the role of external verification.

### Why Existing Work Falls Short
Existing frameworks focus on procedural details like communication flows and buffer updates, failing to clarify the strategic objectives agents stabilize on or the specific quantities optimized by reflection. This leaves three core questions unanswered:
1. How does the orchestrator's decomposition quality control worker coordination?
2. When does unconditional reflection plateau rather than converge?
3. Why do external verifiers succeed where strong text-only critics may fail? Current work lacks analysis of information-theoretic limits in text-indistinguishable environments.

## Core Contributions

### Proposed a Bilevel Coordination Game Framework and Stochastic Reflective Memory Ascent (SRMA)

**Challenge 1: Quantifying the Relationship between Multi-Agent Coordination and Decomposition Quality**
*   **Description & Difficulty**: In multi-agent systems, local updates among workers interact complexly, making it difficult to theoretically quantify how the orchestrator's task decomposition quality affects the overall system's equilibrium state and coordination efficiency.
*   **Solution**: Models the orchestrator-worker interaction as a bilevel coordination game. It proves that under bounded coupling, the workers' local update game is an approximate potential game, with equilibrium slack controlled by decomposition quality, thereby establishing a theoretical link between decomposition quality and coordination stability.

**Challenge 2: Limitations of Pure Textual Reflection and Necessity of External Verification**
*   **Description & Difficulty**: In "text-indistinguishable" environments where semantics are identical but environmental meanings are opposite, gating mechanisms observing only generated text cannot uniformly improve performance, leading to divergence or local optima. Traditional methods struggle to explain the necessity of grounded external verification.
*   **Solution**: Proves an information-theoretic impossibility result: text-only gating cannot improve uniformly across all environments. Consequently, it introduces Stochastic Reflective Memory Ascent (SRMA), which accepts candidate memory only when grounded evaluation risk strictly decreases, proving geometric or polynomial convergence under calibration and non-degenerate corrective mass.

## Implementation and Evaluation

### Implementation and Experimental Setup
*   **Theoretical Instantiation**: The theory is instantiated in hidden-capacity resource contest games, Overcooked with exact BFS value tables, and SWE-bench software engineering projects.
*   **Evaluation Metrics**: Uses environment-grounded metrics to directly expose strategic, memory, and drift quantities, avoiding subjective biases from LLM-as-judge approaches.

### Results and Comparison
*   **SWE-bench Performance**: On 500 SWE-bench instances, the complete Kimi-based system resolved 72.2% (361/500) of problems.
*   **Baseline Comparison**: Outperforms the public mini-SWE-agent reference baseline (70.8%).
*   **Theoretical Validation**: Controlled environment experiments validated the predicted coordination and drift laws, confirming SRMA's convergence properties and the advantage of grounded verification over pure text verification.

#### Summary

This paper provides a unified theoretical framework for multi-agent LLM systems through game-theoretic and information-theoretic lenses. It reveals the critical role of task decomposition quality in coordination and demonstrates the inherent limitations of textual reflection, emphasizing the importance of environment-grounded verification. The proposed SRMA algorithm not only guarantees convergence theoretically but also achieves superior performance on practical software engineering benchmarks compared to existing baselines, laying a theoretical foundation for building more reliable and interpretable multi-agent systems.
