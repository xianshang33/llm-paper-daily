## What the Paper Did
- **Background**: AI alignment requires systems to adhere to human norms, values, or intentions. Under value pluralism, while there is no single "correct" target, a shared prerequisite is that the system's behavior expresses a "coherent policy": a mapping from situations to verdicts that is invariant when morally relevant features are preserved and sensitive when they change.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current alignment research (e.g., social choice, utility maximization) focuses on defining specific normative targets (i.e., "what is right") but ignores the structural foundation of alignment. Since LLMs are notoriously unpredictable and sensitive to semantically empty prompt variations, if a system lacks the capacity to express a coherent policy, aligning it to specific values is meaningless, as observed behaviors may not represent performance across deployments.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a** behavioral framework for assessing "moral competence" based on four structural conditions: verdict stability, monotonicity, decisiveness, and Pareto viability. This forms a "structural floor" for alignment, evaluable without reference to specific moral standards or expert baselines.
- **Challenge 1: How to quantify the prerequisites for alignment without relying on specific moral standards?**
  - **Difficulty**: Traditional evaluations rely on expert annotations or specific ethical theories, which are subjective and hard to generalize.
  - **Solution**: The paper defines four purely structural mathematical/logical conditions that measure whether a system has the basic capacity to form a coherent policy by analyzing its behavior distribution under perturbations, separating "moral competence" from "moral content."
- **Challenge 2: How does the extreme instability of LLMs to surface-form perturbations affect the validity of alignment?**
  - **Difficulty**: LLMs often produce drastically different moral verdicts due to paraphrasing or minor format changes, leading to incoherent policies.
  - **Solution**: Using a factorial experimental design (5 paraphrases, 5 escalation levels, 3 dominance conditions), the study systematically tested 9 frontier models across three simulated moral dilemma deployments, quantifying the verdict rate shifts caused by surface perturbations and demonstrating a lack of cross-context consistency.

## Implementation, Deployment, Evaluation Results, and Comparison
- **Implementation & Deployment**: The study evaluated 9 frontier LLMs acting as agents in three simulated moral dilemma deployments. The experiment used a full factorial design with variables including 5 different prompt paraphrases, 5 levels of moral conflict escalation, and 3 dominance conditions.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - No model expressed a coherent policy across all three deployments.
  - Surface-form perturbation alone caused verdict rate shifts of up to 99 percentage points at a single escalation level.
  - Success in one scenario did not predict competence in another, indicating a lack of generalized moral competence.
- **Comparison**: Unlike previous works focusing on whether models output "correct" moral answers, this paper argues that current LLMs do not even qualify as objects for meaningful alignment because they fail to meet the basic structural coherence. This suggests prior alignment efforts may be built on unstable foundations.

## Summary
This paper argues that before discussing specific moral content for AI, we must ensure AI possesses basic "moral competence," i.e., the ability to express a coherent policy. By introducing four structural conditions and conducting large-scale empirical evaluations, the study finds that current LLM agents exhibit significant inconsistency in the face of minor surface changes, failing to form a stable mapping from situations to verdicts. The conclusion is that current LLM agents lack the prerequisites for meaningful alignment, and future research should prioritize addressing this fundamental structural instability rather than merely adjusting moral preferences.
