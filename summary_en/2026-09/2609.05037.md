## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: As Large Language Models (LLMs) and Artificial Agents (AAs, such as robots and drones) become integrated into smart city environments, they increasingly assume roles requiring moral advice or decision-making. Understanding how Perceived Moral Agency (PMA) is attributed by both humans and LLMs is critical.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: While moral agency is a well-established construct in moral psychology, it remains underexplored in experimental AI research. There is a lack of systematic empirical comparisons between humans and LLMs in evaluating PMA across agents with varying degrees of embodiment, hindering effective ethical governance.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed**: The first empirical study framework comparing how humans and various LLMs evaluate Perceived Moral Agency (PMA) for both human and autonomous artificial agents (with varying embodiment) within plausible smart city scenarios.
- **Challenge 1: Lack of systematic benchmarks for LLM moral evaluation**. The difficulty lies in adapting human-centric moral psychology scales for AI and the absence of direct comparative data. The paper addresses this by adapting a validated PMA scale and applying a unified protocol to 190 human participants and multiple LLMs, enabling cross-subject comparability.
- **Challenge 2: Understanding LLM reasoning preferences in moral dilemmas**. It is difficult to determine whether LLMs rely on stable agent attributes or external contexts. The study reveals that LLMs reason "outward" from the situation, prioritizing harm severity and contextual urgency over stable assessments of the agent itself, thereby amplifying the context-sensitivity also observed in human raters.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Setup**: The study used an adapted Perceived Moral Agency (PMA) scale within smart city scenarios involving human and artificial agents (disembodied AI, robots, drones). Participants included 190 humans and various mainstream LLMs.
- **Evaluation Results**:
    1. Both humans and LLMs perceived higher moral agency in humans than in Artificial Agents (AAs).
    2. LLMs exhibited high context-sensitivity. Contrary to potential fixed biases based on agent type, LLMs focused heavily on situational factors (e.g., severity of harm, urgency).
    3. LLMs not only mirrored certain human judgment patterns but amplified this dependence on context, indicating their reasoning is driven by situational cues rather than stable intrinsic agent properties.
- **Comparison**: As the first study of its kind, it fills the gap in literature regarding direct human-LLM comparison in PMA evaluation. It highlights the unique "outward-looking" context-dependency in LLM moral reasoning, contrasting with views that AI might hold static algorithmic biases.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper presents the first systematic empirical study revealing how LLMs behave when evaluating moral agency. While LLMs, like humans, attribute higher moral agency to humans than to AI, their moral judgments are heavily dependent on external contexts (such as harm and urgency) rather than inherent agent attributes. These findings are significant for understanding the role of LLMs in everyday moral decisions, suggesting that deploying AI for moral judgments requires careful consideration of their sensitivity to contextual variations and potential reasoning biases.
