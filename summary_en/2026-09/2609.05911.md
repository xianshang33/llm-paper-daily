## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Long-horizon LLM agents interact with untrusted content, persistent memory, external states, and sensitive tools, facing threats like indirect prompt injection. Existing analyses typically measure attack risk by the number of execution steps (temporal distance) between malicious input and downstream actions.
- **Limitations of Prior Work**: Existing methods relying on "Sequence Distance" ($D_T$) assume that temporal remoteness implies structural isolation. This is flawed in stateful agents where early malicious inputs can be stored in memory or notes and accessed much later to trigger sensitive actions. In such cases, while the step count is large, the structural data-flow path is short. Relying solely on step counts significantly overstates security separation, failing to accurately identify potential attack paths.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Provenance-aware Execution Graph and Influence Distance ($D_I$)**: This graph connects agent events via deterministic state, identifiers, and tool provenance. $D_I$ is defined as the shortest structural path from an untrusted source to a sensitive action.
- **Challenge 1: How to quantify the overestimation of security isolation by temporal distance?**
  - **Difficulty**: Traditional sequence views ignore direct data dependencies in state and memory, making structurally close attacks appear temporally distant.
  - **Solution**: Defined the gap metric $Gap = D_T - D_I$. Since the influence graph contains all sequence edges, $D_I \\leq D_T$ always holds. The Gap quantifies the structural proximity hidden by the step-count view.
- **Challenge 2: Can structural distance more effectively guide runtime defense?**
  - **Difficulty**: It is necessary to prove that structural metrics capture real attack risks better than temporal metrics and can build more precise defense gates.
  - **Solution**: Experiments show that a pre-execution gate based on $D_I$ intercepts attack sinks missed by sequence-only gates at the same thresholds, without increasing false positives on benign behavior. This demonstrates that execution structure reveals proximity hidden by step counts, supporting more targeted runtime intervention.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Dataset & Models**: Used 360 long-horizon trajectories from the AgentDojo benchmark, covering OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini, gpt-4o, and Claude's Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 4.6. Analyzed 454 injection-sink pairs.
- **Key Results**:
  - **Prevalence of Gap**: $Gap > 0$ for 96.9% of pairs, with a median gap of 9 hops. 91.0% of pairs remain decoupled even after removing the largest class of provenance-only edges.
  - **Cross-domain Decoupling**: In the AgentDojo banking suite, 33.8% of pairs decouple through different provenance mechanisms.
  - **Predictive Power**: After controlling for $D_T$, attack family, and backend, $Gap$ does not independently predict attack success ($p=.088$), indicating $D_T$ remains the primary predictor, but $D_I$ offers a complementary structural perspective.
  - **Defense Comparison**: At matched thresholds $k=2,3$, the deterministic $D_I$-based pre-execution gate blocked 5 attack sinks missed by the sequence-only gate, with no additional benign blocking. Although the paired gain was not statistically significant ($p=.0625$), it demonstrated the potential of structure-aware defense.

#### Summary
This paper highlights the critical issue that "temporal distance" does not equal "structural distance" in long-horizon LLM agent security analysis. By introducing a provenance-aware execution graph and Influence Distance ($D_I$), the authors demonstrate that traditional Sequence Distance ($D_T$) often overstates security isolation. Empirical results show that most attack paths are structurally closer than they appear temporally. Defense mechanisms based on $D_I$ can capture nearby attack paths obscured by step counts, providing a more precise theoretical foundation and practical tool for runtime security intervention in long-horizon agents.
