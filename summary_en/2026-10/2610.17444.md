## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: As Language Models (LMs) are increasingly deployed as agents that observe, reason, and act in external environments, evaluation based solely on outputs fails to diagnose the root causes of agent failures. Suboptimal actions may stem from incorrect state representation, biased preference structures, or a failure to translate available information into action.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current probing methods only establish the availability of task-relevant information in activations, not its functional use in behavior. Meanwhile, behavioral methods like Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) infer preferences but lack a comprehensive framework to decouple "beliefs," "preferences," and the "decision mechanism," making it difficult to distinguish between information absence and belief-action inconsistency.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Belief-Action Gap Analysis Framework**: Systematically studied the interaction of state beliefs, action values, and action preferences in LM agents reasoning and committing to decisions in text-rendered grid worlds.
- **Challenge 1: Distinguishing Information Availability from Behavioral Consistency**
  - **Difficulty**: Successful decoding in traditional probes only proves information exists in hidden layers, not that it drives decision-making, making it hard to determine if failure is due to "not knowing" or "not doing."
  - **Solution**: The paper combines report-based and representation-based probes to estimate beliefs and uses IRL to estimate action values. It finds that task-relevant state and value information is often represented more reliably internally than reflected in behavior, thereby quantifying the belief-action gap.
- **Challenge 2: Understanding Dynamic Decision Formation and Causal Control**
  - **Difficulty**: Chain-of-thought traces may not faithfully reflect computations, and it is unclear how belief changes drive preference shifts; additionally, internal activation edits show limited control over actions.
  - **Solution**: By tracking action preferences throughout reasoning, the study shows decisions sharpen around a "commitment point" with decreased uncertainty, though individual belief changes do not consistently explain preference shifts. Furthermore, transplanting reasoning prefixes from other decisions can force actions, whereas tested activation edits provide limited control, revealing the complexity of the decision mechanism.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Experimental Setup**: Experiments conducted in text-rendered grid worlds using LM agents for observation, reasoning, and action.
- **Evaluation Methods**:
  1. Estimated state beliefs using report-based and representation-based probes.
  2. Inferred latent action values and preferences using Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL).
  3. Tracked the evolution of action preferences throughout the reasoning process.
  4. Conducted intervention experiments, including reasoning prefix transplantation and activation edits within the reasoning window.
- **Key Results**:
  1. **Information Present but Unused**: Task-relevant state and value information is often more reliable in internal representations than in behavioral output, confirming the "belief-action gap."
  2. **Decision Dynamics**: Decisions sharpen near the commitment point with reduced uncertainty, but micro-changes in beliefs do not consistently correlate with preference shifts.
  3. **Intervention Effects**: Forced actions can be achieved by transplanting reasoning prefixes from other decisions, indicating strong guidance by reasoning context; however, direct activation edits have limited control, suggesting simple local interventions are insufficient to master complex decision paths.
- **Comparison**: Unlike works focusing solely on decoding accuracy or single-aspect preference analysis, this study distinguishes information availability from belief-action consistency, highlighting that some failures arise from the translation mechanism rather than information lack.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper reveals the "belief-action gap" in LM agents, where correct state and value information encoded internally is not consistently translated into optimal actions. By decomposing beliefs, preferences, and decision mechanisms, the study finds that decisions become deterministic near the commitment point, yet belief changes lack a simple causal link to preference shifts. Intervention experiments show that reasoning context transplantation influences actions more effectively than local activation edits. These findings emphasize that improving LM agent performance requires not just better information acquisition, but also optimized translation mechanisms from information to action, offering new perspectives for diagnosing and intervening in agent failures.
