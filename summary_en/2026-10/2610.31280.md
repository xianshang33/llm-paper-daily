## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Multi-agent LLM pipelines are often credited with performance gains in software engineering, but it remains unclear whether these gains stem from typed contracts between agents or from specialized prompts enabled by task decomposition.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Previous studies conflate the contributions of architectural mechanisms (like typed contracts) with prompt design. When a pipeline outperforms a single-agent baseline, the gain is often attributed to the contract without verifying if prompting alone would yield similar results, leaving this attribution problem unresolved.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed**: An empirical study framework in Requirements Engineering that disentangles the effects of "decomposition/prompts" from "typed-state QA" by comparing three architectures: a single-call baseline, a two-call variant with unvalidated intermediate states, and a pipeline with Pydantic-validated typed states.
- **Challenge 1: Isolating the source of accuracy improvements.**
  - **Difficulty**: In multi-agent systems, prompt optimization and architectural constraints coexist, making it hard to isolate their individual contributions to F1 scores.
  - **Solution**: By introducing a "two-call variant" that reuses specialized prompts but passes unvalidated intermediates, the study shows that the primary F1 gain (+0.075) comes from this variant, while the typed state adds negligible difference (-0.003 to +0.013), proving that decomposition and prompts drive accuracy, not the typed contract.
- **Challenge 2: Determining the value of typed contracts beyond accuracy.**
  - **Difficulty**: F1 scores fail to capture early error interception or behavioral consistency improvements provided by structural validation.
  - **Solution**: The study analyzes behavior beyond accuracy, finding that typed pipelines catch out-of-range outputs at agent boundaries, whereas unvalidated variants fail later and drop requirements. It also identifies a small but reproducible increase in cross-model MoSCoW consistency.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: Orchestrated using LangGraph with Pydantic for state validation. Experiments covered the PROMISE NFR+ dataset (625 requirements), three locally hosted 7-8B LLMs, and two languages, totaling 18 conditions.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - **Accuracy**: The two-call variant (unvalidated) significantly improved F1 over the baseline (up to +0.075), while the Pydantic-validated typed state had minimal impact on F1.
  - **Quality Assurance**: Typed pipelines effectively intercepted invalid outputs at boundaries, preventing downstream silent failures seen in unvalidated variants.
  - **Consistency**: Typed designs yielded a slight but reproducible improvement in cross-model MoSCoW classification consistency.
- **Comparison**: Unlike prior work that broadly attributes multi-agent gains to architecture, this paper clarifies that accuracy gains are primarily driven by prompts and decomposition, correcting the misconception that typed contracts directly boost accuracy.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper demonstrates through rigorous ablation studies that accuracy gains in multi-agent LLM pipelines are primarily driven by task decomposition and specialized prompts, not typed contracts. However, typed contracts offer distinct value in software quality assurance by providing early structural validation, preventing silent failures, and slightly improving cross-model consistency. The study urges the community to look beyond accuracy metrics when evaluating multi-agent systems, focusing instead on the reliability and robustness contributed by architectural mechanisms.
