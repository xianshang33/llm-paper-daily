## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: In LLM-agent opinion simulations, agents update their probability estimates of a claim being true based on their current state and neighbors' reports. These numerical states feed back into the network, influencing collective behavior evolution.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Prior research focuses on simulation design parameters (e.g., graph structures, update schedules) or robustness to synonymous paraphrasing. However, they overlook a fundamental logical consistency check: if a claim is rewritten as its complement (True becomes False) and probabilities $p$ are replaced by $1-p$, the sum of the two reports should theoretically be 1. Existing works fail to verify if LLMs maintain this mathematical consistency, allowing local wording effects to distort simulation trajectories.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed**: A systematic evaluation framework for "Complement Consistency" in LLM-agent opinion simulations, revealing severe failures in current small open-source models regarding this basic logical constraint.
- **Challenge 1: Systematic bias in LLM probability estimates for complementary claims, violating probability axioms.**
  - **Difficulty**: LLMs often treat probability reports as reactions to specific sentence phrasing rather than beliefs about underlying factual information, leading to non-complementary probabilities for positive and negative formulations of the same fact.
  - **Solution**: Constructed a controlled benchmark of 24 worlds with exactly two mutually exclusive outcomes to isolate complement discrepancies from paraphrase sensitivity. Quantified the extent to which Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B, and Gemma-3-4B produce paired reports summing to more than 1 (average 1.18 for Qwen).
- **Challenge 2: Local inconsistencies amplify over multi-round interactions, breaking symmetry in population simulations.**
  - **Difficulty**: Even with complementary initial states, single-step update inconsistencies cause two mirror populations to diverge rapidly during evolution, rendering simulation results unreliable.
  - **Solution**: Tracked the divergence of two populations starting from complementary states over five rounds, proving positive divergence for all tested models. Proposed a repair method by averaging original answers with their complements; while this restores consistency and lowers average Brier loss, it only outperforms the best original wording in the final round for one of the three models.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Datasets**: Used three small open-source models (Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.2-3B, Gemma-3-4B). Data included 2,304 agent states from running network simulations, 48 real fact-checking claims from AVeriTeC, and a controlled benchmark of 24 binary-outcome worlds.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - **Consistency Failure**: Paired report probabilities significantly exceeded 1 across all tests (Qwen average 1.18). This discrepancy was 2 to 10 times larger than changes caused by synonymous paraphrasing and persisted across repeated sampling, neighbor group sizes, and report strengths.
  - **Population Divergence**: In feedback loops, two populations starting from complementary states failed to remain mirror images after five rounds, showing positive divergence.
  - **Repair Effectiveness**: Enforcing consistency via averaging reduced average Brier loss but only beat the original best wording's performance in the final round for one model.
- **Comparison**: Compared to related work testing only synonymous paraphrase robustness, this paper reveals more severe logical inconsistencies. Experiments showed that while interaction effects are small in fixed-wording schedule experiments, inconsistency becomes the dominant error source when rewriting claims as complements.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper argues that complement consistency under rewording must be tested separately before interpreting LLM simulation probabilities as beliefs. Current small open-source models exhibit severe systematic biases when handling complementary claims. This local inconsistency amplifies through feedback mechanisms, distorting collective simulation trajectories. While simple averaging repairs can restore mathematical consistency, they do not always improve final prediction accuracy, highlighting the importance of distinguishing "wording effects" from "belief updates" in complex simulations.
