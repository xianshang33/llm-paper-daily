## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: LLM-based agents execute tasks by calling tools (e.g., email, payment systems) but are vulnerable to indirect prompt injection attacks that hijack their behavior. Current defenses detect these hijacks by analyzing tool call traces.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: An audit of 11 evaluations revealed that test sets contain disproportionately high attack rates (16%-86%) and lack cross-benchmark testing. This masks real-world challenges: attacks are rare (class imbalance), and tool/task distributions shift over time (distribution shift), causing deployed detectors to perform poorly despite high reported metrics.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed an Evaluation Checklist for Trace Detectors**, exposing three hidden pitfalls in standard evaluations: precision collapse under rare attacks, performance reversal due to unseen tools, and lack of cross-benchmark transferability.
- **Challenge 1: Low Precision Under Rare Attacks**. In real-world scenarios with low attack rates (e.g., 2%), even detectors with high AUROC suffer from extremely low precision due to false positives, which is heavily influenced by the mix of negative samples. The paper demonstrates that relying solely on AUROC is misleading and advocates for precision-recall analysis under varying attack prevalences.
- **Challenge 2: Distribution Shift from Unseen Tools**. Detector performance degrades and rankings reverse when test data contains tools not seen during training, as models overfit to specific tool input features. The paper shows that applying Dropout to tool inputs largely mitigates this reversal, improving generalization to unseen tools.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Experimental Setup**: Tested various detectors (reading tool IDs, behaviors, sequences, embeddings, or text) and the open-source LLM action monitor AlignmentCheck on AgentDrift and AgentDojo benchmarks.
- **Key Results**:
    1. **Impact of Rare Attacks**: At a 2% attack rate, a detector with 0.879 AUROC achieved only 0.076 precision. Precision fluctuated between 0.039 and 0.117 solely based on the negative sample mix.
    2. **Tool Coverage Impact**: As the share of seen tools in test data dropped, detector rankings crossed near 50% coverage and reversed below it. Introducing Dropout on tool inputs largely eliminated this reversal.
    3. **Cross-Benchmark Failure**: Detectors trained on AgentDrift performed no better than chance on AgentDojo (AUROC 0.47-0.52), despite achieving up to 0.996 AUROC within their own domain. The untrained AlignmentCheck monitor reached 0.930 AUROC on AgentDojo but still yielded low precision (0.096) at a 2% attack rate, indicating high false alert rates.
- **Comparison**: Unlike prior works reporting inflated AUROCs, this study highlights that such metrics do not reflect operational security, especially under class imbalance and distribution shifts.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper critiques methodological flaws in current LLM agent hijacking detection evaluations, showing that standard metrics hide poor real-world performance due to ignored class imbalance, distribution shifts (specifically unseen tools), and lack of cross-domain generalization. By systematically demonstrating that high AUROC does not equate to high security, the authors propose a robust evaluation checklist including variable attack rates, tool coverage analysis, and cross-benchmark testing to guide future research and deployment.
