## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Large Language Model (LLM) agents coordinate via conversation, where the same message can be phrased with varying complexity or politeness. Prior research indicates that linguistic form (e.g., formality, persuasiveness) can bias LLM responses and evaluations, such as accepting false claims in sophisticated registers or being misled by persuasive appeals in math problems.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Previous studies largely focus on static evaluations or single-turn interactions, failing to capture how linguistic differences impact judgments and actions in ongoing multi-agent dynamics. Furthermore, natural variations in wording often confound content differences with contextual factors, making it difficult to isolate the causal effect of language style itself.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a controlled experimental framework using LURK Werewolf**: This framework decouples the speech-act predicate (content) from its natural-language realization (form), allowing researchers to rewrite only the linguistic style while preserving game semantics, and replay games from saved checkpoints to isolate stylistic effects.
- **Challenge 1: How to isolate the causal impact of language style on trust and multi-turn decision-making?**
  - **Difficulty**: In dynamic social deduction games, content changes, context history, and language style are intertwined, making it impossible for observational methods to distinguish whether voting changes are driven by content or tone.
  - **Solution**: The authors employed a control variable approach, keeping semantic content constant while systematically manipulating the speaker's "linguistic range" (vocabulary/syntax complexity) and per-utterance "politeness strategies." They replayed thousands of interactions from identical game states to statistically test for significant effects.
- **Challenge 2: Do different roles adopt distinct linguistic strategies in natural gameplay?**
  - **Difficulty**: There is a lack of large-scale, fine-grained analysis of natural dialogue to verify theoretical assumptions (e.g., whether Werewolves are more deceptive/polite than Villagers).
  - **Solution**: Analysis of natural play data revealed that 88% of face-threatening acts are performed "bald on-record" (directly), regardless of role. This suggests that in high-stakes social deduction, efficiency outweighs social etiquette, and no significant politeness strategy differences exist between roles.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: The study utilized the LURK Werewolf framework, conducting 30 full games and 4,335 replays. Two dimensions were manipulated: consistent "linguistic range" (restricted, neutral, expanded) and per-utterance "politeness strategies."
- **Evaluation Results**:
  1. **Trust**: Neither linguistic range nor politeness strategies significantly altered the trust other agents placed in the speaker.
  2. **Voting & Actions**: After correction for multiple comparisons, no effects on votes or night actions were statistically significant. The largest uncorrected effect showed that shifting away from neutral paraphrasing changed only about 4 percentage points of other players' votes.
  3. **Natural Behavior**: In natural gameplay, the vast majority (88%) of face-threatening speech was direct, with no discernible difference in politeness strategies across roles.
- **Comparison**: Contrary to prior work suggesting strong biases from linguistic style, this study demonstrates that LLM agents in complex, logic-driven multi-agent environments are robust to surface-level stylistic manipulations, prioritizing substantive content over form.

## 4. Conclusion
This paper reveals through rigorous controlled experiments that linguistic form (complexity and politeness) has a negligible impact on final decisions and trust building among LLM social deduction agents. The findings indicate that agents prefer direct communication in competitive settings and are not easily manipulated by language style. These results challenge assumptions about the persuasive power of linguistic style in complex interactions, highlighting the importance of distinguishing content from form when evaluating LLM social behaviors.
