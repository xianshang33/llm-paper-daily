## What the Paper Does

### Background
LLM-based verifiers are increasingly used to evaluate agent trajectories, providing process-level feedback beyond final outcomes. However, in deployed systems, execution traces are often abstracted through composed tools (macro actions), delegation boundaries, or history compaction, altering the evidence observable to the verifier.

### Why Existing Work Falls Short
Current reliability studies typically assume verifiers access a fixed, native representation of the trajectory. This overlooks the "abstraction boundaries" prevalent in real-world deployments. Consequently, measuring accuracy only on native views is insufficient, as it remains unclear whether correct verifier judgments persist when execution evidence crosses these abstraction boundaries.

## Core Contributions

### Proposed Verification Transport Framework
The paper introduces "Verification Transport," a reliability test that measures the stability of verifier judgments under abstraction-induced changes in execution observability.

### Challenge 1: Judgment Instability Due to Abstraction
**Challenge**: Abstraction (e.g., macro actions) hides intermediate steps, causing verifiers to fail due to limited observability, a difficulty stemming from the lack of robustness in traditional verifiers to partial information.
**Solution**: By constructing matched native, abstracted, and restored views of frozen executions, the paper quantifies transport failure rates. It reveals that direct verification often yields incorrect verdicts, while evidence-grounded protocols tend to abstain, highlighting distinct failure modes.

### Challenge 2: Distinct Failure Modes and Recovery
**Challenge**: Different verification protocols exhibit varying behaviors under information loss, making it difficult to equate low failure rates with high reliability without understanding the nature of the failures (error vs. abstention).
**Solution**: The study analyzes these modes and demonstrates that restoring execution provenance recovers 85.5% of transport failures, proving that preserving or recovering underlying execution details is crucial for maintaining verification reliability.

## Implementation, Evaluation, and Comparison

### Experimental Setup
The evaluation spans four agent benchmarks, seven verifier backbones, four verification protocols, and three abstraction boundaries, ensuring all variables except observability remain constant.

### Key Results
1. **High Transport Failure**: Failure rates reach ~31% under macro abstraction and delegation, indicating significant instability.
2. **Failure Mode Differences**: Direct verification tends to produce incorrect verdicts, whereas deliberative protocols abstain. This suggests lower failure rates do not necessarily imply greater reliability.
3. **Provenance Recovery**: Restoring execution provenance recovers 85.5% of failures, though recovery is lower with lossy or unavailable provenance.

### Comparison
Unlike prior work focusing solely on native-view accuracy, this study demonstrates that such metrics are insufficient for characterizing reliability across abstraction boundaries. It exposes the fragility of the implicit assumption in existing literature that execution representations are fixed.

## Summary

This paper highlights a critical gap in LLM agent evaluation: the stability of verifier judgments across abstraction boundaries. By introducing the "Verification Transport" metric, it confirms that current verifiers suffer significant reliability drops when facing trajectory abstraction. The findings emphasize that native-view accuracy alone is inadequate for assessing verifier reliability in deployed systems. Restoring execution provenance is identified as an effective strategy to enhance robustness, offering key insights for building more reliable agent evaluation frameworks.
