## 1. What the Paper Does
- **Background**: In distributed systems, different clients (e.g., laboratories) may measure the same unknown physical law but cannot pool raw observations, and each covers different input ranges. The goal is to recover a single compact global analytic equation from this dispersed evidence by exchanging only equations and aggregate scores.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Existing symbolic regression methods (e.g., PySR, LLM-SR) typically assume centralized data or use fixed workflows. In distributed settings, locally accurate expressions may only be approximations of the global law within specific ranges. Current federated symbolic regression focuses more on parameter inference rather than adaptive tool selection for coordinating symbolic search across clients, struggling to handle discrepancies in data distribution.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed Query-ATLAS**: A query-conditioned Agentic Tool-based Learning framework for Aggregating Symbolic Equations. It uses an LLM as a controller to guide symbolic search in a simulated distributed system, dynamically selecting tools for profiling, structural proposal, local symbolic regression, and cross-client scoring based on client errors and previous proposals.
- **Challenge 1: How to integrate local discoveries from different input coverage ranges to recover the global law without sharing raw data?**
  - **Difficulty**: Local data may only reflect part of the global law or an approximation, leading to bias if models are simply merged.
  - **Solution**: Query-ATLAS exchanges candidate expressions and aggregate fit scores via an equation-level interface. The LLM controller adaptively adjusts the search strategy based on cross-client scoring feedback to identify global equations supported across all clients.
- **Challenge 2: How to balance the computational cost of adaptive search with discovery accuracy?**
  - **Difficulty**: Adaptive tool selection introduces additional LLM inference overhead, potentially making it significantly more expensive than fixed workflows without guaranteed performance gains in all tasks.
  - **Solution**: The paper reveals task-dependent benefits through comparative experiments, showing that adaptive selection significantly improves validation pass rates in complex tasks (e.g., oscillator models) while fixed workflows perform better in others, highlighting the need for task-aware tool selection.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation**: Implemented in a simulated distributed environment with an LLM coordinator and multiple client workers. Evaluated on 12 Feynman benchmarks, two client distributions (overlapping and partitioned support), and three seeds, totaling 360 runs.
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - With overlapping support, Query-ATLAS exactly recovered 23 out of 36 equations, compared to 21 for local PySR with cross-client selection. The two additional recoveries involved relativistic mass.
  - With partitioned support, Query-ATLAS recovered 18, while local PySR recovered 19.
  - In a post-hoc analysis of 72 settings, Query-ATLAS achieved 64 validation passes and 64 in-distribution passes, versus 62 and 59 for the fixed workflow. Both passed 47/60 out-of-distribution tests.
  - Query-ATLAS had a mean runtime about five times higher than the fixed workflow, which retained lower median errors and costs.
- **Comparison**: Compared to the fixed PySR-LLM workflow, Query-ATLAS significantly improved validation acceptance rates in specific tasks (e.g., CRK3 and oscillator2) but performed worse in others (e.g., MatSci3). This indicates that adaptive coordination is not universally superior but offers broader query coverage as a potential benefit.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper investigates whether adaptive tool selection helps identify shared equations in distributed observations. The proposed Query-ATLAS framework demonstrates better equation recovery capabilities than fixed workflows in certain tasks, particularly for complex physical laws with overlapping data support. However, it also exposes limitations in computational cost, runtime, and performance under partitioned data support. The conclusion emphasizes that the benefits of scientific priors are limited, there is a gap between accurate approximation and equation recovery, and future work should focus on task-aware tool selection and client-aware fitting rather than claiming general superiority of adaptive methods.
