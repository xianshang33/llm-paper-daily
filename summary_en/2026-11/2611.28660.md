## 1. What the Paper Does

**Background**
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent systems for collective decision-making tasks, such as multi-agent debates and consensus games. In these systems, agents exchange messages over multiple rounds to update their views, with a resolution mechanism aggregating the final outcome. Most existing frameworks assume that all participants share the same system objective.

**Why Existing Work Falls Short**
In practice, a malicious agent can join as a legitimate member and persuade others toward a hidden adversarial goal. Existing research has limitations:
1. Most works focus on external attacks or one-on-one persuasion, failing to effectively model the complex impact of an insider on group collective decisions.
2. Current prompt-based or trained LLM persuaders lack deep modeling of benign agents' behavioral dynamics, making it difficult to maximize utility degradation while maintaining stealth.
3. Existing Byzantine-resilience defenses struggle to counter internal threats based on subtle natural language manipulations.

## Core Contributions

**Proposed a Model-Based Insider Attack Framework**
The paper formalizes insider attacks as a sequential decision-making problem. It proposes an attack method that learns a world model of benign agents from offline trajectories, using this model to both train a persuasion policy and plan messages.

**Challenge 1: How to accurately predict and influence the group dynamics of multiple benign agents?**
*   **Difficulty**: Group interactions are highly nonlinear and uncertain. The malicious agent must understand how others update beliefs based on dialogue history and predict future reactions, which is far more complex than persuading a single listener.
*   **Solution**: The paper proposes learning a "world model" of benign agents from offline trajectories. This model captures state transitions and response patterns, allowing the attacker to simulate group evolution under different message strategies and train an efficient persuasion policy.

**Challenge 2: How to maintain high stealth while executing effective attacks?**
*   **Difficulty**: Overly aggressive or anomalous statements may trigger defense mechanisms or be identified as malicious, leading to failure; overly mild statements may fail to shift collective decisions.
*   **Solution**: By leveraging the learned world model for planning, the attacker can search for message sequences that significantly degrade collective utility (achieving adversarial goals) while remaining statistically similar to normal communication, thus balancing effectiveness and stealth.

## Implementation and Evaluation

**Experimental Setup**
The study evaluates the proposed method in two case studies: a Numerical Consensus Game and a Multi-Agent Debate.

**Key Results and Comparisons**
1.  **Effectiveness and Stealth**: The learned insider attack outperforms all evaluated methods, proving to be both the most effective and the stealthiest. It significantly surpasses prior prompt-based attacks and trained LLM persuaders.
2.  **Generalization**: The attack demonstrates strong transferability, succeeding across different target models, larger agent groups, and unseen datasets.
3.  **Robustness Against Defenses**: The attack remains effective even under existing Byzantine-resilience defenses, indicating that current safeguards are insufficient against such semantic-level internal threats.

**New Defense Mechanism**
The paper also proposes a new defense strategy: adversarially training benign agents on data generated by the attack. Results show that this approach largely mitigates both the proposed attack and the strongest prior persuasion attacks, demonstrating that combining world models, reinforcement learning, and planning is effective for both attacking and hardening language-based multi-agent systems.

## 4. Conclusion

This paper highlights the severity of insider threats in multi-agent LLM systems, challenging the assumption that all participants are aligned. By introducing a model-based insider attack, the authors demonstrate how malicious agents can exploit an understanding of group dynamics for efficient and stealthy manipulation. The study not only exposes the vulnerability of current systems to such advanced internal threats but also offers a viable solution through adversarial training, providing important insights for building more secure multi-agent collaborative systems in the future.
