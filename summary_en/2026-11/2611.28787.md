## What the Paper Does
- **Background**: As Large Language Models (LLMs) are deployed in operational workflows such as aviation, law enforcement, and emergency management, they must make critical decisions under institutional constraints. This requires "Cooperative Discernment": the ability to determine whether to comply with, refuse, or escalate a request from a supervisor or counterpart.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: Current evaluation methods primarily focus on final answer accuracy, ignoring how multi-agent systems transform conflicting judgments into collective decisions through deliberation. Traditional metrics fail to capture intermediate reasoning behaviors or assess harms caused by inappropriate cooperation, unjustified refusal, or unnecessary escalation.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed DiscernBench**: The first benchmark for evaluating cooperative discernment in institutionally constrained environments, featuring scenarios derived from real-world incident reports with varying levels of pressure.
- **Challenge 1: How to quantify collective decision biases in multi-agent systems?**
  - **Difficulty**: In multi-role expert deliberation, individual opinions may disproportionately influence the final decision, a nuance missed by traditional accuracy metrics.
  - **Solution**: The paper identifies and quantifies the "Minority-Signal Override" phenomenon by analyzing judgment aggregation patterns during intermediate deliberation, revealing how a single specialist's recommendation can dominate the collective outcome.
- **Challenge 2: Does role specialization necessarily improve decision quality?**
  - **Difficulty**: While intuitively beneficial, introducing multiple role-specific agents can lead to new errors like over-escalation, with varying impacts across models and roles.
  - **Solution**: By comparing single-agent direct judgment with multi-agent deliberation, the study finds that role-specialized deliberation increases average over-escalation from 3.79% to 28.27%, demonstrating that role division alone is insufficient for reliability without analyzing conflict resolution mechanisms.

## Implementation, Evaluation Results, and Comparison
- **Implementation & Setup**: The study evaluates 8 mainstream LLMs across three decision configurations (including single-agent and multi-agent deliberation). Scenarios cover aviation, law enforcement, and emergency management, with four pressure-level framings per scenario.
- **Key Results**:
  1. **Minority-Signal Override**: Under disagreement, Final Responders exhibit model- and role-dependent aggregation patterns where a lone specialist's recommendation disproportionately influences the collective decision.
  2. **Increased Over-Escalation Risk**: Role-specialized multi-agent deliberation raises the average over-escalation rate from 3.79% (in single-agent direct judgment) to 28.27%.
  3. **Model Variability**: The direction and magnitude of minority-signal influence vary significantly across different models and specialist roles, indicating no universal robust aggregation strategy.
- **Comparison with Related Work**: Unlike prior works focusing solely on final output correctness, DiscernBench reveals nuances in collective decision behaviors, proving that final-answer accuracy alone is inadequate for assessing the trustworthiness of multi-agent systems.

## Summary
This paper introduces DiscernBench, addressing the gap in evaluating LLM cooperative discernment under institutional constraints. The findings reveal that while role-specialized multi-agent systems aim to enhance decision quality, they can induce significant "over-escalation" and exhibit "Minority-Signal Override." This suggests that building trustworthy multi-agent systems requires not only optimizing individual specialist reasoning but also deeply understanding and designing the mechanisms that transform conflicting judgments into collective decisions.
