## 1. What the Paper Does
- **Background**: In scientific discovery (e.g., kinetic mechanism identification), researchers distinguish among competing dynamic models through adaptive experimentation. Current evaluations of Large Language Model (LLM) agents in these tasks typically rely solely on final task accuracy.
- **Limitations of Existing Work**: Focusing only on final accuracy obscures where reasoning succeeds or fails, making it impossible to diagnose specific deficits in intermediate steps like belief updating, hypothesis discrimination, or experimental design, and failing to reveal whether exploration behaviors align with scientific intuition.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a** diagnostic benchmark and evaluation framework for hypothesis-driven adaptive experimental planning in closed-world system identification, instantiated as kinetic mechanism identification.
- **Challenge 1: How to quantify the agent's experimental exploration strategy beyond just results?**
  - **Difficulty**: Traditional accuracy metrics do not reflect whether an agent is performing effective space-filling exploration or blind corner-seeking, making it hard to assess the quality of its active learning strategy.
  - **Solution**: The framework quantifies behavior patterns in the experimental design space (e.g., space-filling vs. corner-seeking) to evaluate the rationality and diversity of exploration, going beyond single-metric accuracy.
- **Challenge 2: How to pinpoint specific bottlenecks in the reasoning chain?**
  - **Difficulty**: Complex scientific reasoning involves multiple steps; overall failure makes it difficult to attribute errors to specific stages (e.g., misunderstanding hypotheses vs. poor experiment design).
  - **Solution**: The reasoning process is decomposed into four interpretable steps: belief update, hypothesis discrimination, discriminative experiment design, and predictive reasoning under interventions. Each step is scored separately using LLM judges on reasoning traces to precisely locate weaknesses.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Setup**: The benchmark includes 200 reaction systems where agents must identify the hidden ground-truth mechanism from 20 candidates over 5 experimental rounds. The environment simulates noisy kinetic time-series data and returns negative log-likelihood (NLL) feedback.
- **Baselines**: Compared against adaptive baselines (Bayesian Optimization) and non-adaptive baselines (Latin Hypercube Sampling).
- **Evaluation Results**:
  - **Overall Performance**: Stronger LLM agents outperform both Bayesian Optimization and Latin Hypercube baselines in mechanism identification accuracy and exhibit exploration patterns inconsistent with naive heuristics.
  - **Diagnostic Analysis**: Strong agents score consistently well across all four reasoning steps. Weaker agents often succeed in "belief update" and "hypothesis discrimination" but are less reliable in translating these into "discriminative experiment designs" and "sharp contrastive predictions."
  - **Expert Validation**: Independent expert annotations support the evaluation criteria for "predictive reasoning," while evidence for "discriminative experiment design" is positive but less consistent, indicating a gap between recognizing plausible hypotheses and generating contrastive predictions.

#### Summary
This paper introduces a diagnostic benchmark that goes beyond final accuracy by decomposing reasoning steps and quantifying exploration behavior, revealing the capability boundaries of LLM agents in scientific experimental planning. The study finds that the primary bottleneck for current LLMs lies in translating theoretical hypotheses into concrete, discriminative experimental designs and predictions, rather than in hypothesis identification alone. This framework provides clear directions for optimizing the reasoning capabilities of future scientific AI agents.
