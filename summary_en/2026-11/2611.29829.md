## 1. What the Paper Did
- **Background**: Agentic pipelines combining graph detectors, deterministic rules, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and LLM judges are increasingly used to monitor illicit cryptocurrency flows and generate regulation-citing justifications.
- **Why Existing Work Falls Short**: Current evaluations focus on end-to-end accuracy or justification quality, failing to identify which component actually made the decision. A fluent justification does not imply the explaining component was the decision-maker, creating an accountability gap.

## Core Contributions
- **Proposed a Decision-Source Attribution Audit Framework**: Applied a trace-level protocol with three layers (per-case actual causation, interventional Shapley credit, and mediation of upstream risk scores) to audit an agentic pipeline on the Elliptic++ Bitcoin dataset.
- **Challenge 1: Identifying the true decision-maker in multi-agent systems**. Difficulty lies in disentangling the roles of interacting components (graph models, rules, retrievers, verifiers, LLM). The method reveals that a deterministic wallet-score override carries ~94% of the credit for flags, while a verification gate cancels most of the judge's erroneous flags on licit transactions, showing the LLM judge is not the primary decider.
- **Challenge 2: Determining if the LLM judge decides based on evidence or upstream bias**. Difficulty arises because evidence gathered with visible scores may still encode those scores even if hidden later. Tests show the LLM judge follows injected risk scores, fails to verdict with insufficient context, and cites inapplicable regulations, proving its justification is not evidence of its decision source.

## Implementation and Evaluation
- **Implementation & Evaluation**: Audited a pipeline with investigator agent, compliance agent, LLM judge, entailment gate, and deterministic override on Elliptic++. The judge was tested alone across 76 evidence bundles, 4 backbones, and 2 prompts.
- **Results**:
    1. Deterministic wallet-score override dominates decisions (~94% credit).
    2. LLM judges heavily rely on upstream risk scores; weaker models remain biased even with explicit facts, while stronger models perform better when facts are clear.
    3. Short contexts lead to no verdict.
    4. Substring-based checks fail to detect inapplicable regulatory citations.
- **Comparison**: Unlike traditional metrics focusing on accuracy or fluency, this study exposes that high performance can mask the LLM's lack of causal role, highlighting blind spots in current accountability assessments.

## 4. Conclusion
The paper argues that an LLM's justification in illicit activity monitoring should not be interpreted as evidence of which component flagged a transaction. Fine-grained attribution reveals that deterministic rules often drive decisions, while LLM judges suffer from bias dependency and citation hallucinations. This has significant implications for building accountable AI systems in financial compliance.
